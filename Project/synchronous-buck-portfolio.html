Synchronous Buck Converter

Power Electronics  |  PWM  |  Feedback Control Development

Independent Project  ·  2026  ·  In Progress

This project explores the design and characterization of a synchronous DC–DC buck converter, with a longer-term goal of implementing regulated output-voltage feedback. I am developing the power-stage design around a complementary MOSFET half-bridge, an output LC filter, and a controlled PWM switching signal. The work combines converter analysis, component and gate-drive considerations, waveform measurement, and an investigation of how best to close the control loop.

Project status: Power-stage analysis and PWM/measurement work are underway. The final feedback implementation has not been selected or validated. I am comparing analog, dedicated digital, and microcontroller-based approaches before committing to the next revision.

1. Converter Architecture

A buck converter reduces a DC input voltage to a lower DC output by rapidly switching the input across an inductor and using a capacitor to smooth the resulting output voltage. Rather than continuously dropping the excess voltage across a linear pass device, the power MOSFETs primarily operate in on and off states, allowing efficient conversion.

The synchronous version replaces the conventional freewheeling diode with a second, actively controlled MOSFET. The two transistors form a half-bridge whose midpoint is the switch node, VSW. The inductor connects this node to the output, while an output capacitor and load are connected from the output to ground.

DC inputVin and local capacitors
MOSFET half-bridgeQH, QL and gate driver
LC output filterInductor and capacitor
Load / measurementVout and IL

The eventual feedback controller will measure the output voltage, compare it with a reference, and adjust the PWM duty ratio. At this stage, the power-conversion hardware and the selection of the control platform are treated as separate design problems so that the converter can be characterized before the final compensation scheme is chosen.

2. Why Use a Synchronous Buck Converter?

In an asynchronous buck converter, a diode conducts the inductor current while the high-side switch is off. The diode is simple and does not require a second gate signal, but its forward-voltage drop produces conduction loss. Replacing the diode with a low-side MOSFET can reduce that loss, particularly when the load current is large enough for the diode's forward drop to become significant.

The tradeoff is increased complexity. Both MOSFET gates must be driven correctly, particularly during switching transitions. If the devices turn on simultaneously, they create a low-impedance path from the input supply to ground. The gate-drive design must therefore include appropriate dead time between switching events.

High-Side On Interval

When QH is on and QL is off, the switch node is approximately at the input voltage. The inductor sees approximately Vin − Vout, so its current increases:

dIL/dt ≈ (Vin − Vout)/L

Low-Side On Interval

When QH is off and QL is on, the switch node is pulled near ground. The inductor voltage is approximately −Vout, and its current decreases:

dIL/dt ≈ −Vout/L

Under ideal steady-state continuous-conduction conditions, the average inductor voltage must be zero over a switching period. This volt-second balance leads directly to the familiar ideal buck relationship, Vout ≈ D Vin.

3. PWM Frequency and Duty-Ratio Control

The main control input to the buck converter is the PWM duty ratio, D. It represents the fraction of the switching period during which the high-side switch is commanded on. With a constant switching frequency, changing D adjusts the average voltage applied to the output filter.

D = ton/Ts    and    Ts = 1/fs

For a 50kHz switching frequency, the switching period is 20µs. As an illustrative timing calculation, a 30% high-side duty ratio corresponds to a 6µs commanded on interval, while 70% corresponds to 14µs. Actual gate waveforms must also account for propagation delays and intentionally inserted dead time.

fs = 50kHz ⇒ Ts = 20µs
D = 0.30 ⇒ ton = 6µs
D = 0.70 ⇒ ton = 14µs Illustrative command timing, not a substitute for measured MOSFET gate timing.

Exploring Timer-Based PWM

As part of related converter experimentation, I worked with an Arduino Uno using the ATmega328P's 16-bit Timer1 to generate a 50kHz PWM waveform. A hardware timer is more suitable for repeatable high-frequency switching than toggling an output pin using software delays, because the timer continues operating independently of ordinary application-code execution.

At a 16MHz timer clock and a prescaler of one, a 50kHz period contains 320 clock counts in a single-slope PWM configuration. That offers a useful duty-ratio resolution for initial experiments. However, producing the complementary, properly interlocked high-side and low-side drive signals is a separate requirement; a single PWM pin is not by itself a safe synchronous gate-drive solution.

4. Selecting the Output Inductor and Capacitor

Inductor Current Ripple

The inductor stores energy while the high-side switch is on and releases energy while the low-side device conducts. Its value sets how quickly the current changes each cycle. For an ideal converter operating in continuous conduction, the peak-to-peak inductor-current ripple is approximately:

ΔIL,pp ≈ [(Vin − Vout)D] / (L fs)

A larger inductance reduces ripple current but can increase component size, DC resistance, and transient-response time. A smaller inductance allows the current to change more quickly, but produces larger ripple and may increase core and switching stresses.

In my earlier ECE 469 converter experiments, I compared 2mH and 300µH inductors to examine how inductance affects ripple, operating mode, and response. Those were course-laboratory measurements informing this independent design; they are not being presented here as measurements of the final standalone converter.

Continuous and Discontinuous Conduction

In continuous-conduction mode (CCM), the inductor current remains continuous across the switching period. With a diode-based buck, the current can reach zero at light load, entering discontinuous-conduction mode (DCM). A synchronously switched low-side MOSFET can instead allow reverse inductor current if it continues conducting after the current crosses zero. This changes light-load behavior and may reduce efficiency if current is unnecessarily circulated back toward the input.

Output Capacitor Selection

The output capacitor supplies the difference between the inductor current and load current, reducing switching ripple at Vout. For a simple ideal CCM approximation, neglecting equivalent series resistance (ESR):

ΔVout,pp ≈ ΔIL,pp / (8fsC) The capacitor's ESR also introduces a ripple contribution, approximately ΔIL,pp × ESR.

Choosing the capacitor therefore involves capacitance, ESR, ripple-current rating, voltage rating, and the effective capacitance under DC bias. The inductor and capacitor must ultimately be selected together for the intended load and control-bandwidth targets.

5. Gate Drive, Dead Time, and Device Stress

A synchronous converter requires the high-side and low-side MOSFETs to switch in a controlled sequence. The gate driver must provide sufficient current to charge and discharge the MOSFET gate capacitances and, for an N-channel high-side device, must establish an appropriate gate voltage relative to the moving switch node.

Dead time is deliberately inserted between turning one MOSFET off and turning the other on. It prevents shoot-through caused by finite gate-discharge and device switching times. However, excessive dead time has its own cost: during part of the interval, the inductor current may freewheel through a MOSFET body diode, increasing conduction loss and potentially worsening reverse-recovery behavior.

Gate-drive tradeoff: Too little dead time risks simultaneous conduction; too much dead time raises body-diode conduction losses. The appropriate setting should be checked using actual gate and switch-node waveforms rather than chosen only from nominal MOSFET datasheet timing.

Device selection must account for blocking voltage, peak and RMS currents, RDS(on), total gate charge, switching energy, and thermal resistance. The worst-case stresses occur at operating points that may differ from the point of maximum output power. I treat the MOSFET and driver ratings as a combined design problem, especially as switching frequency increases.

6. Conduction Losses, Switching Losses, and Efficiency

The ideal relationship Vout = D Vin neglects losses. In practical testing, the measured output can fall below this ideal prediction, particularly as load current rises and the semiconductor and inductor voltage drops become more important. Earlier buck-converter experiments also showed why ideal duty-ratio calculations need to be compared against actual waveforms and input/output power measurements.

MOSFET Conduction Loss

When a MOSFET is on, it behaves approximately like a small resistance. As a first approximation, conduction power is proportional to the square of RMS current:

Pcond ≈ IH,rms2RDS(on),H + IQ_L,rms2RDS(on),L

Switching Loss

During turn-on and turn-off, a MOSFET can simultaneously support substantial drain-source voltage and current. One rough hard-switching estimate is:

Psw ≈ ½ VinIsw(tr + tf)fs A first-order estimate only; measured switching energy and diode recovery may alter the result substantially.

Conduction and switching losses contribute to total heating. Additional losses include inductor winding resistance and core loss, output-capacitor ESR, body-diode conduction during dead time, gate-drive power, and other parasitics. The converter's efficiency is calculated from measured input and output power:

η = Pout / Pin × 100%
Pout ≈ VoutIout

The design therefore involves a compromise: higher switching frequency can reduce the required filter size, but generally increases switching-related losses; lower switching frequency reduces the number of transitions per second but can require larger passive components to maintain the same ripple.

7. Measuring the Converter and Preparing for Feedback

A closed-loop converter needs an accurate representation of its output. The most direct feedback variable for output-voltage regulation is Vout, which can be compared with a desired reference voltage Vref. An embedded controller would typically measure a scaled voltage through an ADC, while an analog controller would compare the voltage using a continuous-time error amplifier.

Scaling a Voltage for an ADC

When the output voltage exceeds the controller's ADC input range, a resistor divider can scale it down. Ignoring ADC loading:

VADC = Vout · Rbottom / (Rtop + Rbottom)

Calibration is necessary because resistor tolerances, sensor gain, offsets, and ADC reference error can affect the reported value. A practical check compares the digitized reading against a multimeter or oscilloscope and fits a gain (and, if necessary, an offset) before using it in a feedback loop.

Inductor-Current Measurement

Current sensing is useful for observing conduction mode, calculating ripple, enforcing current limits, and evaluating current-mode control. It is not strictly required for basic voltage-mode PI control, but it becomes important for protection and more advanced control strategies.

In related Arduino-based laboratory work, I investigated sensor scaling and compared voltage/current telemetry against instrument readings. That experience informs the measurement architecture of this project, but the final sensor interface and calibration values for the independent converter remain to be set.

8. Hardware and PCB Layout Considerations

The PCB design is shaped by the current paths that change most rapidly. In a synchronous buck, the high-frequency commutation loop includes the local input capacitors and the high-side/low-side MOSFET pair. Keeping this loop compact reduces stray inductance, ringing, and voltage overshoot at the switch node.

The MOSFET gate traces and their returns should also be short and well-defined. Gate resistance, driver current, and layout inductance together influence switching transitions. The switch-node copper region requires a balance: sufficient width for current and thermal handling, but not so much area that unnecessary capacitive coupling and noise increase.

Low-level sense wiring should be kept away from switching nodes and the high-current power loop where practical. Input and output capacitors should be positioned to minimize their relevant current-loop paths. After assembly, measured switch-node ringing and MOSFET temperatures are useful feedback for revising the layout.

9. Characterization Approach and Observations

My evaluation focuses on confirming switching behavior before adding a closed-loop controller. The key measurements are Vin, Vout, the PWM duty command, switch-node voltage, inductor current, and input/output power. Together they show whether the power stage behaves as predicted and where practical losses appear.

Duty-Ratio Sweep

A useful first characterization is to change duty ratio at a fixed input voltage and load, then plot measured Vout against the ideal prediction D Vin. The difference can help identify nonideal behavior such as MOSFET and inductor conduction drop, switching loss, dead-time effects, or changes in operating mode. A larger discrepancy does not uniquely identify any one loss mechanism; separate measurements are needed to isolate causes.

Switching and Current Waveforms

Oscilloscope captures of the switch node and inductor current are useful for checking switching period, edge behavior, current ripple, and whether inductor current approaches or crosses zero. I also compare gate-drive transitions to confirm that the two MOSFETs are not commanded on simultaneously.

The discussion above combines converter-theory predictions with experience from earlier course experiments. No unverified standalone-prototype efficiency, regulation, or load-step results are claimed here.

10. Feedback Control — Work in Progress

The next phase is to select the feedback implementation that best balances regulation performance, complexity, flexibility, and available hardware. I am currently investigating analog, digital hardware, and embedded microcontroller control rather than treating one approach as already finalized.

ApproachPotential advantagesTradeoffs to investigate
Analog
Compensator / controller IC		 Continuous-time response, no ADC quantization or firmware execution delay, established PWM control architectures Compensation component selection, tuning flexibility, additional analog circuitry
Digital hardware
FPGA or dedicated digital controller		 Deterministic timing, precise PWM/dead-time logic, configurable control structure Development complexity, sensing interface, fixed-point precision and resource use
Embedded
MCU / ATmega328P-class		 Software-adjustable gains, logging, telemetry, straightforward iteration ADC sampling rate, computation latency, PWM resolution, interrupt timing, reliable complementary gate generation

Embedded control is itself a form of digital control; I distinguish it here from dedicated digital hardware because their timing, development, and integration tradeoffs are different.

Candidate Voltage-Mode Feedback Loop

Regardless of implementation, a voltage-mode controller would compare Vout against a reference Vref and adjust the duty command. In a sampled PI implementation, for example:

e[k] = Vref − Vout[k]
I[k] = I[k−1] + KiTse[k]
D[k] = sat(Kpe[k] + I[k]) Illustrative controller form only; no final gains or closed-loop performance are claimed.

Duty-cycle limiting and anti-windup are important considerations, because an integrator can continue accumulating error when the actuator is saturated. The controller's sampling rate must also be chosen with the switching frequency, converter poles, phase margin, ADC resolution, and processing delay in mind.

Before selecting a final approach, I plan to characterize the duty-to-output response, define voltage-regulation and transient-response targets, and compare each implementation's achievable bandwidth, power consumption, protection features, and design complexity. This control comparison is the active next stage of the project rather than a completed result.
